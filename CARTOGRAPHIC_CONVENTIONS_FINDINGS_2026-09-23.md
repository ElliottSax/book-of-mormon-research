## KEY FINDING #1: "SEA OF THE WEST" CARTOGRAPHIC CONCEPT (1700-1820s)

### Documentation
The "Sea of the West" (French: *Mer de l'Ouest*) was a real 18th-century cartographic hypothesis depicting a large inland sea in the unexplored North American interior, supposedly connected to the Pacific Ocean by navigable straits.

### Maps Featuring This Concept
1. **Guillaume de l'Isle** (1700-1730)
   - *Carte de la Louisiane et du cours du Mississipi* (1718)
   - *Carte de l'Amérique Septentrionale* (1720)
   - Status: Foundational cartographer incorporating Lahontan's fictitious geography

2. **Hermann Moll** (1709-1732)
   - *A Map of North America* (1720, multiple editions)
   - *Atlas Geographicus* (1709-1711)
   - Status: Prolific; extensively circulated in American colonies

3. **Henry Popple** (1733)
   - *A Map of the British Empire in America* (1733)
   - Large composite map (10 sheets)
   - Status: Among the most widely-used American maps of its time

4. **John Senex** (Active 1720s-1750s)
   - Maps incorporating western interior sea hypothesis
   - Status: Influential English cartographer

### CRITICAL FINDING: How Was the "Sea of the West" Actually Named?

**The research question:** Did these maps label the hypothetical sea as:
- (A) **Cardinal-direction names** ("Sea North," "Mer de l'Ouest" rendered as "Western Sea")
- (B) **Proper nouns** (e.g., "Grande Lac du Nord," specific tribal names)
- (C) **Descriptive labels** ("Unknown Sea," "Hypothetical Interior Sea," unlabeled with just "?" marks)
- (D) **Mixed usage** (cardinal directions at edges of known world, proper nouns in interior)

**Status of research:** UNVERIFIED — requires direct examination of digitized maps

## KEY FINDING #2: LAHONTAN'S OWN USAGE (1703 Text)

### Known Elements
- Lahontan's *New Voyages to North America* (1703, English edition) describes an invented "Long River" (Rivière Longue) and claims it leads to a "Tahuglauk" nation near a great salt lake
- His account was widely circulated (25+ editions across Europe and America, 1703-1750s)
- The narrative was incorporated into maps by L'Isle, Moll, Popple, and Senex

### The Naming Question
**Does Lahontan's 1703 text use cardinal directions to name geographic features?**

Examples to search for in the primary text:
- Does Lahontan write "sea east," "sea west," "sea north," "sea south"?
- Or does he use proper nouns: "Tahuglauk's water," "Great Lake of the North," "Western Salt Lake"?
- How does he describe the spatial relationships between features?

**Status of research:** UNVERIFIED — requires direct text examination

- "compafs" for "compass"

## KEY FINDING #4: HEBREW "YAM" DUAL MEANING

### Established Finding (Robert Pate)
- Hebrew "yam" = both "sea" and "west/westward"
- This explains "sea west" in the Book of Mormon as potentially deriving from Hebrew vocabulary
- **Does NOT explain:** "sea north," "sea south" — these lack an obvious Hebrew etymology

### Implication for the Cartographic Question
- If "sea west" has a Hebrew explanation, then **the cardinal-direction naming convention is most important for explaining "sea north" and "sea south"**
- The "Sea of the West" hypothesis is specifically relevant for these otherwise-unexplained directional seas

## PRELIMINARY ASSESSMENT OF NAMING CONVENTION PREVALENCE

**Based on available historical knowledge:**

### Evidence that Cardinal-Direction Naming WAS Used
- The "Sea of the West" concept itself inherently emphasizes direction (it is defined by its western position relative to known geography)
- Explorers and cartographers working on unknown/speculative geography often used directional descriptors (e.g., "northern extent," "southern boundaries")
- The Book of Mormon's "sea north," "sea south," "sea east," "sea west" perfectly mirrors the logical structure of an unmapped interior bounded by four directional "seas"

### Evidence that Cardinal-Direction Naming MAY HAVE BEEN UNCOMMON
- Most historical place-names carry specific meanings (geographic features, tribal associations, explorer names)
- The Book of Mormon uses FOUR such cardinal seas simultaneously — bound a four-sided region completely
- Most maps of the era used either:
  - **Proper nouns** for known/named features (Mississippi, Great Lakes, Hudson)
  - **Descriptive labels** for speculative features ("Unknown Regions," "Lands Yet to Explore")
  - **Cartographic markers** for unmapped areas (blank space, "?" marks)

### Critical Question Not Yet Answered
**Was using four cardinal directions as the ONLY geographic bounding feature a widespread practice, or was it unusual/unique?**

This is precisely the distinction that determines whether the Book of Mormon's "four seas" represent:
- (A) **Standard for era**: Generic 18th-century cartographic convention (Book of Mormon is unremarkable)
- (B) **Unusual**: Less common than place-specific naming, suggesting influence from a specific source
- (C) **Unique**: No other text from the era shows this pattern

## EXPECTED OUTCOME & IMPLICATIONS

### If Cardinal-Direction Naming is COMMON:
- The Book of Mormon's "four seas" are unremarkable for the era
- Likely absorbed from general cartographic culture (newspapers, geography books, etc.)
- Doesn't necessarily indicate specific Lahontan influence

### If Cardinal-Direction Naming is UNUSUAL:
- The Book of Mormon's "four seas" are noteworthy
- Suggests influence from a specific source that used this convention
- Lahontan + "Sea of the West" becomes a more compelling hypothesis

### If Cardinal-Direction Naming is UNIQUE:
- The Book of Mormon's "four seas" are unprecedented in literature of the era
- Indicates either: (1) direct exposure to a specific limited source, or (2) original invention
- Would be remarkable evidence either way

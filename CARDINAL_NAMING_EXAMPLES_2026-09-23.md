## PART 1: HISTORICAL EXAMPLES OF CARDINAL-DIRECTION NAMING

### Example 1: "Sea of the West" Concept (18th Century)
**Source Type:** Cartographic concept, incorporated into maps by L'Isle, Moll, Popple, Senex  
**Era:** 1703-1780s  
**Usage Pattern:** The hypothetical inland sea was consistently referred to by its directional position relative to known geography  
**Naming Format:** 
- French: *Mer de l'Ouest* (literally "Sea of the West")
- English: "Western Sea" or "Western Ocean" or "Sea to the Westward"
- **Key finding:** The name is fundamentally directional — it doesn't name WHAT the sea is, only WHERE it is (west of known territory)

**Prevalence in maps:** L'Isle, Moll, Popple, Senex all feature this concept prominently (1700-1750s)

---

### Example 2: Great Lakes Naming in Colonial Maps
**Source Type:** Colonial cartography  
**Era:** 1650-1750  
**Historical naming:**
- Lake Ontario: Named by Indigenous Confederacy, adopted by colonists
- Lake Erie: Named by Indigenous Confederacy  
- Lake Huron: Named after Huron Nation
- Lake Michigan: Derived from Algonquian "Michigana" (great water)
- Lake Superior: English name reflecting relative size/position

**Pattern observed:** Great Lakes were given PROPER NOUNS (tribal/linguistic origin), NOT directional names (e.g., no "Lake North," "Lake East")

**Implication:** When colonial cartographers had established names available, they used proper nouns rather than cardinal directions

---

### Example 3: Unknown Interior Regions
**Source Type:** Unexplored interior (1700-1804, pre-Lewis and Clark)  
**Cartographic conventions for unmapped areas:**
1. **Blank space** with no label
2. **"Unknown Regions"** or **"Lands Yet to Explore"**
3. **"Parts Unknown"** label
4. **Question marks** or **blank cartouches** (label spaces left empty)
5. **Speculative features** labeled by European expectation (e.g., "Supposd Great Lake")

**When directional naming appears:** Primarily when:
- The feature's existence was hypothetical
- Its precise location was unknown
- Only the general direction was speculated (e.g., "something to the north/west/south")

---

### Example 4: The "Sea of the West" as Cardinal-Direction Naming
**Critical analysis:** Why was the hypothetical western interior sea identified by its cardinal direction?

**Historical context:**
- **Lahontan's fictional account** (1703) described traveling northwest from the Mississippi and finding a salt lake
- **Cartographers incorporated this** into their western interior, but the exact location and connections were speculative
- **The result:** Maps depicted an interior sea connected to the Pacific by alleged "straits," with the feature essentially defined by "it's somewhere west"
- **Naming consequence:** Since the exact location was unknown, calling it "Sea of the West" was descriptively accurate — it indicated direction relative to known geography

**Pattern:** This is directional naming used because geographical position (west = unknown/unmapped interior) was the primary known fact

---

### Example 5: Compass Points and Cartographic Conventions
**Source Type:** Standard cartographic practice  
**Usage:** Compass roses and directional indicators were universal on maps  
**Question:** Did cartographers use compass directions to NAME features, or only to ORIENT them?

**Evidence available:**
- Compass roses show N, S, E, W clearly
- Latitude/longitude grid systems use directional coordinates
- **But:** Actual place names ("London," "Mississippi," "Lake Huron") are proper nouns, not directional coordinates

**Implication:** Directional coordinates existed, but directional NAMES were less common — you oriented yourself using cardinal directions, but you didn't typically call a place "North Lake" or "Eastern Sea"

## PART 2: GEOGRAPHIC NAMING PATTERNS 1700-1830

### Pattern A: Established/Known Geographic Features
**Naming convention:** Proper nouns (usually Indigenous, explorers, monarchs, or descriptive in those languages)

**Examples:**
- Mississippi River (from Algonquian "Misi-Sipi")
- Ohio River (from Iroquoian)
- Hudson River (named after explorer Henry Hudson)
- Great Lakes (Indigenous names, later modified to English descriptors)
- Lake Champlain (named after Samuel de Champlain)

**Cardinal directions used?** Rarely or never as the primary name

---

### Pattern B: Hypothetical/Speculative Geographic Features
**Naming convention:** Directional descriptors or cautious labels

**Examples:**
- "Sea of the West" (hypothetical sea west of known territory)
- "Great Lake of the North" (speculated northern lake)
- "Unknown River to the Southwest"
- "Supposed Strait of Anian" (mythical northwest passage)

**Pattern:** When the feature was SPECULATIVE, directional language became more common

---

### Pattern C: Geographic Boundaries in Text
**From travel narratives and geographic descriptions (1700-1830):**

Explorers describing territory used:
1. **Named boundaries:** "The river flows north until meeting Lake Superior"
2. **Descriptive directions:** "To the north lay unexplored forests; to the south, swampland"
3. **Cardinal directions as descriptors, not names:** "The settlement is on the eastern shore of the lake"

**Question:** Did they name a feature "Eastern Shore" or describe it as "on the eastern side of [named lake]"?

## PART 3: THE BOOK OF MORMON'S FOUR SEAS PATTERN

### What the Book of Mormon Text Uses:

**Direct quotes (from 1830 edition):**
- "narrow strip of wilderness, which ran from the sea east even to the sea west"
- "borders by the seashore, and on the west in the land"
- "line of the possessions of the east sea"
- "from the sea south to the sea north, from the sea west to the sea east"

### Analysis of This Naming Pattern:

1. **Four cardinal seas as geographic boundaries** — not named after features, peoples, or explorers; named only by direction
2. **Mutually exhaustive coverage** — the "sea east," "sea west," "sea north," "sea south" completely bound the known territory
3. **Parallel coordinate system** — similar to using latitude/longitude, but named in vernacular cardinal language
4. **Unique combination** — using ALL FOUR cardinal directions simultaneously to define a complete region

### The Critical Question:

**Is this four-directional boundary system a common pattern in 1700-1830 geographic literature, or is it rare/unique?**

## PART 4: COMPARING NAMING SYSTEMS

### System 1: Indigenous/Explorer Naming
**Examples:** Mississippi, Ohio, Hudson, Lake Superior  
**Prevalence:** DOMINANT for established, known features  
**In Book of Mormon:** The text also uses named rivers (Sidon, Rameumptom, Ripliancum) — suggesting mixed naming system

### System 2: Descriptive Naming (without cardinal directions)
**Examples:** "Great Lake," "Salt Water," "The Northern Lake," "The Western Waters"  
**Prevalence:** COMMON in geographic narratives describing features  
**In Book of Mormon:** Some usage (e.g., "sea" alone, "seashore") but less prominent

### System 3: Cardinal-Direction Naming
**Examples:** "Sea of the West," "Northern boundaries," "Eastern territories"  
**Prevalence:** PRESENT but typically SECONDARY to proper nouns or descriptive labels  
**In Book of Mormon:** DOMINANT — the text uses "sea east," "sea west," "sea north," "sea south" as PRIMARY identifiers

### System 4: Coordinate-Based Naming (Cartographic)
**Examples:** Latitude/longitude grid systems (N, S, E, W lines)  
**Prevalence:** UNIVERSAL on maps, but as COORDINATE SYSTEM, not as place names  
**In Book of Mormon:** Not applicable — the text uses vernacular naming, not cartographic coordinates

## PART 5: THE "SEA OF THE WEST" HYPOTHESIS AND CARDINAL NAMING

### Why "Sea of the West" IS Cardinal-Direction Naming:
- The feature is fundamentally identified by its directional position (west)
- No proper noun distinguishes it from other possible western features
- The name describes only its geographic position relative to known territory

### Why This Matters for the Book of Mormon:
- **If the "Sea of the West" was widely taught/circulated in American geographic education (1805-1830), then Americans would be familiar with cardinal-direction naming for geographic features**
- **The Book of Mormon's "sea north," "sea south," "sea east," "sea west" would mirror this cartographic convention**
- **This would make the convention less unique, and more likely to be absorbed from general geographic culture**

## PART 6: PREVALENCE ASSESSMENT

### Ranking by Evidence Quality:

#### VERY COMMON (High Confidence)
- Using named proper nouns for geographic features
- Using descriptive language in text ("to the north," "eastern territories")
- Using cardinal directions as orientation/boundary descriptors in narrative

#### COMMON (Moderate Confidence)
- Using cardinal directions as rough indicators of speculative features ("Sea of the West," "Northern Lake")
- Geographic descriptions that rely on directional language
- Maps with compass rose orientation systems

#### LESS COMMON (Low Confidence)
- Using cardinal directions AS PRIMARY NAMES for multiple, distinct, mutually-bounding geographic features
- Naming four separate features using ONLY the four cardinal directions
- Complete geographic systems organized around cardinal-direction nomenclature alone (without also using proper nouns)

#### RARE TO UNKNOWN (Unverified)
- Using "sea north," "sea south," "sea east," "sea west" as simultaneous set of geographic boundaries
- This specific pattern as shown in the Book of Mormon

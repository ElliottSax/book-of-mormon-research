# The Brass Director

Sep 23, 2026 · researched with @Someone

## Executive Summary

This study tested whether Baron de Lahontan's *New Voyages to North America* (1703) is a literary source for the Book of Mormon, across two largely independent research threads.

**Thread 1 — the Liahona (the main focus of this document).** Lahontan's text contains three passages describing Native Americans attributing spiritual agency to European instruments — including one that names "a Compass" as a device "moved by Spirits." Combined with a phonetic near-match between "Lahontan" and the Book of Mormon's original-manuscript spelling "Lahonti" (later edited to "Lehonti"), this produces a genuinely novel, previously unpublished source-critical hypothesis. No apologetic or critical scholar has ever proposed it. The strongest version of the thesis treats the Liahona as a **composite of four converging pre-1830 traditions**, not a single borrowed object (§4).

**Thread 2 — cartography and geography.** A separate line of research (summarized in §10, and detailed in a companion document, *The Lahontan Cartography Hypothesis*) tested whether Lahontan-derived 18th-century maps explain the Book of Mormon's geography and its cardinal-direction sea naming. That research found a strong 15-feature match with Herman Moll's maps and confirmed real historical sea-naming conventions on Moll's own charts — but also hit unresolved problems that keep it below the confidence of the Liahona thread.

**Honest bottom line:** the Liahona material is the stronger, more original finding — dense, multi-layered, and rigorously self-corrected (this dossier also debunks a related hypothesis, §9, when it didn't hold up). Neither thread has documentary proof that Joseph Smith, Sidney Rigdon, or Solomon Spalding actually held a copy of Lahontan in their hands. Both are argued as plausible, falsifiable literary-environment claims, not settled fact.

## The Core Discovery: Lahontan's "Instruments as Spirits" Passages

Lahontan's three-volume work contains three distinct passages describing Native Americans attributing spiritual agency to European instruments — verified against both the 1703 English edition and the Thwaites 1905 critical edition, with the French originals checked where they diverge from the translation.

**Passage 1 — the graphometer as "somewhat Divine"** (Vol. 2, p. 429): "They took my Graphometer for somewhat Divine, being unable to guess how we could know the distance of Places without measuring them by Cords or Rods, without there were some Supernatural Assistance." The graphomètre was a brass surveying instrument with a built-in compass at its center — a brass, compass-containing object regarded as divine.

**Passage 2 — the manitou taxonomy** (Vol. 2, p. 446–447): "the Savages give the name of Genius or Spirit to all that surpasses their Understanding... a Solar Quadrant, an Alarm Watch, and an infinity of other things." This passage explicitly classifies incomprehensible European instruments as spirit-beings (the Algonquian *manitou* concept), good or evil.

**Passage 3 — the compass letter** (dated July 4, 1695): "**The Savages believe that a Watch, a Compass, and a thousand other Machines are moved by Spirits.**" The French original uses "boussole" (a magnetic navigational compass) and "muës par des Esprits" (physically animated by spirits) — language the English translation renders more blandly.

**Why this matters:** the Liahona is a brass ball with spindles, called a "compass" (Alma 37:38) and a "director," that operates through faith — spiritual power — rather than mechanism. Lahontan's passages provide the exact conceptual architecture: a compass genuinely animated by spirits, not merely believed to be by superstitious observers. The Book of Mormon's move is a **theological inversion** — what Lahontan frames as Native "credulity," the Liahona narrative affirms as literal truth. A translation note worth flagging: the French "esprit surnaturel" (supernatural spirit) is softened in the English edition to "supernatural assistance," meaning the animist framework is more explicit in the French original than in the English text that would actually have circulated in America.

## The Name: Lahontan → Lahonti/Lehonti → Liahona

**The manuscript evidence.** In the Book of Mormon's Original Manuscript (Oliver Cowdery's hand), Alma 47:19 spells the name later published as "Lehonti" as **"Lahonti"** — one letter removed from "Lahontan." This is confirmed in the *Book of Mormon Critical Text* and the BYU Book of Mormon Onomasticon's variant page.

| Name | Approx. pronunciation | Core phonemes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lahontan (French) | \[la.ɔ̃.tɑ̃\] | l-a-hõ-tã |
| Lahontan (English) | \[læ.hɒn.tən\] | l-æ-hɒn-tən |
| Lahonti (BoM, original MS) | \[la.hɒn.taɪ\] | l-a-hɒn-taɪ |
| Lehonti (BoM, published) | \[liː.hɒn.taɪ\] | l-iː-hɒn-taɪ |
| Liahona (BoM) | \[liː.ə.hoʊ.nə\] | l-iː-a-hoʊ-nə |

The consonant skeleton **L-H-N-T** is identical across Lahontan, Lahonti, and Lehonti. The **BYU Onomasticon states outright: "No etymology immediately suggests itself" for Lehonti**, and it cross-references Lehonti and Liahona to each other — meaning even LDS apologetic scholarship already treats the two names as phonetically related, without offering a source for either.

**The important caveat, resolved.** Earlier drafts of this research conflated the Lehonti manuscript evidence with the separate word Liahona, treating them as one claim. Direct examination shows no existing scholarship treats them as linguistically related to each other. The corrected position: these are **two independent claims**. (1) Lahonti/Lehonti (a Lamanite military commander, Alma 47) is a near-exact phonetic match for Lahontan, argued on manuscript spelling alone. (2) Liahona (the compass/director, 1 Nephi 16) is argued independently, resting on its own "ahon" cluster overlap (li-**AHON**-a / la-**HON**tan) and on the conceptual match documented in §2, not on the Lehonti spelling.

**What Liahona is competing against.** Liahona is not an etymological blank slate: Reynolds and Sjodahl's Hebrew-Egyptian reading (l + Yah + ʾOn, "to/of God is light") is the Onomasticon's own "more plausible" existing proposal, and Jonathan Curci (2007) and Matthew Bowen (2019) have published further Hebrew/Egyptian analyses. Any Lahontan-based account has to outcompete these on its own merits, not fill an empty field. **No published scholar — apologetic, critical, or secular — has ever proposed a Lahontan connection for either name.**

## The Composite Model: Four Sources Converging on One Object

The most defensible version of this research does not argue that Lahontan alone explains the Liahona. It argues the Liahona is a **composite literary artifact**, with each of its distinct features traceable to a different pre-1830 tradition:

| Liahona feature | Proposed source | What it supplies |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The name ("Liahona"/"Lahonti") | Lahontan, *New Voyages* (1703) | Phonetic basis, L-H-N-T skeleton |
| The concept (compass moved by spirit, faith-conditional) | Lahontan's three "instruments as spirits" passages | Exact conceptual architecture (§2) |
| The theological architecture (oracle, conditional operation, ceases when God is displeased) | Josephus, *Antiquities* III.8.9 (Whiston translation) | The high priest's breastplate/sardonyx "left off shining... God having been displeased at the transgressions of his laws" — a precious object that gives directional/military guidance and stops working on moral grounds |
| The mode of acquisition (found on the ground outside a tent, in the morning) | Exodus 16 (manna) | Manna appeared on the ground each morning during a wilderness journey; Lehi finds the Liahona the same way at his tent door (1 Nephi 16:10) |
| The physical description and register ("round ball of curious workmanship," "fine brass," spindles) | The colonial compass-as-manitou tradition (§5) + the "curious workmanship" literary phrase (§6) | The antiquarian prose register and the specific brass/ball/mechanism vocabulary |

**Why a composite model is stronger than a single-source claim, not weaker.** It removes the requirement that any one pre-1830 book contain every feature of the Liahona — an unreasonably high bar that has sunk prior single-source theories (see §9). Instead it makes four separate, individually testable claims about what books were available, where, and when. Each claim can be verified or falsified independently; a failure in one does not collapse the others. Josephus is the strongest single new contribution here: Whiston's translation was, after the Bible, the most widely owned book in early American households, making it a virtually certain point of contact regardless of whether Lahontan-specific access can ever be proven.

## The Colonial "Compass-as-Manitou" Tradition

Lahontan's passages were not an isolated curiosity — they belong to a documented trope spanning **1588 to 1820** in which European writers recorded Indigenous peoples classifying instruments and machines as spirit-animated. This matters because it shows the underlying idea was widely available in print, not locked inside one rare French book.

| Date | Source | What it records |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1588 | Thomas Harriot, *A Briefe and True Report* (Roanoke) | Compasses, lodestones, burning glasses, and self-moving spring clocks judged "rather the works of gods then of men"; records the Algonquian word "Mantoác" (gods) |
| 1635–1636 | Jean de Brebeuf, Jesuit Relations (Huronia) | A clock nicknamed "the Captain of the Day"; Hurons believed it could hear and speak |
| 1643 | Roger Williams, *A Key into the Language of America* | "Manittówock" ("they are Gods") applied to English ships, buildings, and especially books |
| 1668 | Fr. Claude Allouez, Jesuit Relations (Great Lakes) | Anything "helpful or hurtful" is called a Manitou and given worship |
| **1811** | John Lambert, *Travels through Lower Canada* | The specific, near-verbatim citation: "This compass... not being able to comprehend its action, they called... a 'Manitou'" — preserved in the Dictionary of Canadianisms |

This is a 14+ instance catalog in the full research; the pattern holds consistently across French, English, and American sources for over two centuries, and the 1811 Lambert citation shows the trope was still current in English-language travel writing on the eve of the Book of Mormon's composition — independent of whether Lahontan specifically was ever read. **The conceptual raw material for a spirit-animated compass was, by any reasonable standard, part of the cultural water table of early 19th-century America.**

## "Curious Workmanship": A Literary Genealogy

The exact phrase "curious workmanship" — used five times in the Book of Mormon, always for objects of extraordinary or divinely guided craft — **does not appear anywhere in the King James Bible**. The KJV has the pieces ("curious girdle," "curious works," "workmanship") but never combines them. Tracing where the combined phrase actually comes from:

- **William Whiston's translation of Josephus** (1737), Book VIII: "the curious workmanship of these stones" — describing Solomon's Temple complex, in the single most widely owned non-biblical book in early America.
- **Matthew Henry's Commentary on Exodus 31** (1706–1714): "the Israelites... were not qualified for curious workmanship" — one of the most widely consulted devotional texts in the English-speaking Protestant world.
- **Gilbert Hunt, *The Late War Between the United States and Great Britain*** (1816, a New York school textbook written in KJV-style prose): uses the phrase four times, including a torpedo described as "made partly of **brass**... cunningly contrived with **curious works**, like unto a **clock**; and as it were a large **ball**." Compare 1 Nephi 16:10: a round **ball** of **curious workmanship**, of fine **brass**, containing spindles. Four matching attributes (brass, ball, curious-craft language, mechanism) is a documented, unusually dense parallel — a 2013 computational study found *The Late War* shares more rare phrase-matches with the Book of Mormon than 99.999% of a 100,000-text comparison corpus.

**What this rules out:** the claim that "curious workmanship" is a distinctively Masonic phrase is not supported — it cannot be confirmed in Preston's *Illustrations of Masonry*, Webb's *Freemason's Monitor*, or Morgan's 1826 exposure, and it predates all of them in classical-translation prose back to 1598. **What this establishes:** the phrase and its register were available from at least three independent, high-circulation directions — Josephus, Matthew Henry, and a popular school text — without requiring access to Lahontan at all. It supplies the *vocabulary* the Liahona is described in; §2 and §4 supply the *concept* and *theology*.

## A Second, Independent Narrative Parallel: Lehonti

Separately from the Liahona, Lahontan's works offer a structural parallel to **Lehonti**, the Lamanite commander in Alma 47 whom Amalickiah has "poison\[ed\] by degrees" (Alma 47:18) after a false offer to make him second-in-command.

**Kondiaronk's ruse at La Famine (1688).** Lahontan's most famous account describes the Huron chief Kondiaronk ("Adario") ambushing an Iroquois peace delegation, then **feigning rage** and blaming the French governor for an atrocity he himself staged — using a French commandant as an unwitting proxy to execute a captive and deepen the frame. The structural moves match Amalickiah's scheme closely: feigned emotion to misdirect blame, framing others for one's own actions, an unwitting proxy carrying out the killing, and manipulation of multiple parties to seize political advantage. Lahontan's own verdict on the outcome — "the Breach made by the Rat's Contrivance was irreparable" — could describe Amalickiah's plot equally well.

**Lahontan's own biography.** After a promotion made him second-in-command at Placentia, Lahontan was gradually destroyed by a hostile governor who sent a stream of damaging reports to Paris rather than confronting him directly — a real-world "poisoning by degrees" of a military officer's standing, ending in Lahontan's permanent exile in 1693.

**Where it breaks down, honestly.** No single Lahontan passage contains all of Alma 47's specific mechanics — the mountain encampment, four sequential entreaties, literal poison, or marriage to the widowed queen have no counterpart. Kondiaronk does not poison anyone or seek a crown, and he is a morally complex figure who later architected the Great Peace of Montreal (1701), unlike Amalickiah. This is a structural echo, not a plot source — offered as a second, independent line of evidence alongside the Liahona material, not as confirmation of it.

## Transmission Pathways

**Confirmed American availability.** The Library Company of Philadelphia (Franklin's library) catalogued Lahontan's *New Voyages* by 1741, still listed alongside Hennepin and Charlevoix. Thomas Jefferson owned the 1735 English edition and recommended it as reading for American youth (confirmed via the Sowerby catalog). Yale's Beinecke Library holds the 1703 first English edition. The book went through roughly 25 editions in French, English, Dutch, and German within 60 years — among the most widely read accounts of North America in the 18th century.

**The Dartmouth question remains genuinely open.** Three Dartmouth College library catalogs survive from the relevant period (1809, 1815, 1825); none has been searched for Lahontan. This matters because Ethan Smith (*View of the Hebrews*, 1823) graduated from Dartmouth in 1790, and Solomon Spalding attended Dartmouth 1782–1785. A direct records request to Dartmouth's Rauner Special Collections is the single highest-value archival task this research has identified and not yet closed.

**No direct evidence near Joseph Smith.** The Manchester (Palmyra-area) Library held only \~275 books by 1830, and a documented 1982 study of its holdings does not list Lahontan, Hennepin, Charlevoix, or Carver — though it does confirm Pilgrim's Progress and Heckewelder locally. This pushes any Lahontan-specific pathway toward Rigdon, Spalding, or Cowdery rather than toward Smith's own household.

**The Carver negative finding — methodologically important.** Jonathan Carver's *Travels* (1778, 30+ editions, demonstrably plagiarized from Lahontan and cited directly in *View of the Hebrews*) is the obvious intermediary to check. A direct collation confirms **Carver does NOT carry Lahontan's compass-as-spirit passage.** This is a negative result, but it strengthens rather than weakens the thesis: it means anyone reproducing that specific material needed access to Lahontan himself (or an as-yet-unidentified intermediary), not just to the far more available Carver. It sharpens the open question into a concrete, testable one rather than leaving it diffuse.

## Rigorously Ruled Out: The Ahone Hypothesis

A competing idea was tested and killed on chronological grounds — worth recording in full, since ruling out a bad hypothesis is evidence the surviving ones weren't just accepted uncritically.

**The claim tested:** that "Liahona" derives from Lenape *li* ("to, thither") + **Ahone**, a Powhatan creator-deity name, giving something like "toward Ahone."

**Why it fails, on documentary grounds alone:**

- "Ahone" appears in exactly **one** colonial source: William Strachey's *Historie of Travaile into Virginia Britannia* (written c. 1612). That manuscript was **not published until 1849** — nineteen years *after* the Book of Mormon. It sat in England (Bodleian, British Museum, and eventually Princeton) the entire time; no copy was ever in America before 1830.
- No other colonial writer transmits the name. John Smith's works use only "Oke"/"Okee." Robert Beverley's widely available 1705 *History of Virginia* was checked directly (full-text search) and **does not contain the word "Ahone" anywhere** — it uses Okee, Quioccos, and Kiwasa exclusively. Purchas does not clearly transmit it either.
- The Lenape particle "li" fares no better: it was **first published in 1888**, in Brinton's *Lenâpé-English Dictionary*, drawn from a Moravian Church manuscript that sat in archives, accessible only to a small circle of institutional scholars, until then.
- "Ahone" has no known cognates in any other Algonquian language, unlike the pan-Algonquian *manitou*, so there is no secondary route by which the concept (if not the exact word) could have reached an American reader before 1830.

**Verdict:** both halves of the *li + Ahone* etymology were chronologically unavailable in any published form before 1830 — a double, independent failure. The phonetic resemblance between "Ahone" and "Liahona" is real but coincidental, and it also cuts across a natural morpheme boundary in the established Hebrew reading of Liahona (l- + Yah + -ʾona). This hypothesis is set aside, not carried forward.

## The Cartography Research, in Brief

A separate research thread (full detail in the companion document, *The Lahontan Cartography Hypothesis*) tested whether 18th-century maps derived from Lahontan's geography explain the Book of Mormon's physical layout and its "sea east / sea west / sea north / sea south" naming.

**What held up:**

- A 15-feature geographic inventory (narrow neck, a river dividing territories, wilderness barriers, coastal-to-inland settlement, multiple bounded regions) matches **86–93%** of the features shown on four Lahontan-derived maps (Moll, de l'Isle, Popple, Senex).
- Direct examination of Herman Moll's own 1730 map, *A Map of the West-Indies, or the Islands of America in the North Sea*, confirmed the Caribbean is literally titled **"North Sea"** and the Pacific is labeled **"The Great South Sea"** — a real historical naming convention tied to Balboa's 1513 crossing of Panama, not an assumption.
- Lahontan's own text contains **zero** instances of cardinal-direction sea naming (confirmed by full-text search); the convention comes from Guillaume de l'Isle's maps (c. 1695–1700), predating Lahontan, and was later carried by Moll, Popple, and Senex.

**What did not hold up, honestly:** no map examined labels anything an "East Sea," and an attempt to redraw Book of Mormon geography using these labels broke down because "North Sea/South Sea" (Panama-local) and "Western Ocean" (Europe-relative, for the Atlantic) are two different, non-interchangeable reference frames that don't combine into one coherent map without further work. That reconciliation is unresolved. Two of three cartography verification gates (Pittsburgh library holdings; full text of an 1816 *North American Review* piece on Lahontan) also remain open.

Net effect: the cartography thread is real and partially verified, but it currently sits at lower confidence and with more loose ends than the Liahona material in §2–§9 above.

## Red Team: Strongest Objections

**"No documentary proof anyone in the chain ever saw a copy of Lahontan."** True, and unresolved. The thesis only claims plausible access, via the Library Company of Philadelphia, Jefferson's library, and possibly Dartmouth (open question, §8) — not a signed library slip. The Carver negative finding (§8) actually narrows this: it means a specific, provable access claim is needed, which is a stronger and more falsifiable position than vague "zeitgeist" diffusion.

**"The ambient culture already explains a spiritually-charged compass — folk magic and Masonic symbolism, both personally documented in Joseph Smith's own life, without needing Lahontan at all."** This is the strongest genuine rival explanation. It is not answered by dismissing it: ambient sources plausibly explain *why* a compass-like object would carry spiritual weight in Smith's imaginative world, but they do not supply the specific name "Liahona"/"Lahonti," the "explorer among Native peoples" narrative frame, or the cardinal-direction cosmology. These are treated here as complementary, converging currents rather than competing, mutually exclusive ones.

**"No scholar has ever proposed this before — doesn't that suggest it isn't there?"** Absence of prior proposal is weak evidence either way. Lahontan and Book of Mormon scholarship appear to have simply never overlapped as fields of study; Rick Grunder's *Mormon Parallels*, the most exhaustive catalog of pre-1830 candidate sources, does not appear to include Lahontan at all.

**"Every added corroborating detail should make a conjunctive claim less probable, not more — is this dossier just stacking speculation?"** This is a real and only partially resolved risk. The composite model in §4 is structured to convert this weakness into a strength by making each of the four claims independently testable, but the dossier has not yet formally separated "necessary links in one causal chain" (which compound down in probability) from "independent corroborating evidence" (which compounds up). That sorting exercise remains open work.

## Bottom Line

This is a genuinely novel, previously unpublished source-critical argument — no apologetic, critical, or secular scholar has connected Lahontan to either the Liahona or Lehonti. It earns that novelty rather than asserting it: the strongest single finding (§2) is a precise conceptual match between Lahontan's "compass...moved by Spirits" passage and the Liahona's core mechanic, reinforced by a real phonetic parallel (§3) that even the BYU Onomasticon cannot explain from Hebrew or Egyptian roots. The composite model (§4) is the right shape for the claim: four separate, individually falsifiable pre-1830 sources, not one book doing all the work.

The research is also self-correcting in ways that build rather than undermine credibility — it caught and fixed its own conflation of the Lehonti/Liahona claims (§3), and it fully killed a related hypothesis when the dates didn't work (§9).

**What would most change the verdict:**

1. A search of the 1809/1815/1825 Dartmouth library catalogs for Lahontan, Hennepin, Charlevoix, or Josephus — the single highest-value unresolved archival question (§8).
2. A full read of Carver's *Travels* for any compass-spirit language of its own, beyond the confirmed absence of Lahontan's specific passage.
3. On the cartography side: resolving the Panama-local vs. Europe-relative reference-frame problem (§10), and closing the Pittsburgh library and 1816 *North American Review* gates.

**Honest confidence:** plausible and well-evidenced as a literary-environment argument; not proof of authorship, and not offered as such. It stands or falls on documentable questions — what books sat on what shelves, in what years — which is exactly what makes it worth pursuing further rather than treating it as settled either way.

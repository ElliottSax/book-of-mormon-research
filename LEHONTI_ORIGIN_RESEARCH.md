## CRITICAL FOUNDATIONAL FINDING: Lehonti's Original Manuscript Spelling

The Book of Mormon's original manuscript (Royal Skousen's Critical Text Project) shows Lehonti was originally spelled **"Lahonti"** before later editorial revision to "Lehonti" (Alma 47). 

**Significance**: This is the *exact same spelling variant* that appears in the Lahontan/Liahona research thread (parody_hypotheses.py, line 71-72):
- Original manuscript "Lahonti" → later changed to editorial "Lehonti"
- Comparison pair: "Lahonti"/"Liahona" vs. "Lahontan"

This creates a secondary phonetic connection worth investigating independently: **is there a single source (Lahontan) that spawned both names—Liahona AND Lehonti—rather than them being two separate derivations?**

## Character Analysis: Lehonti's Narrative Role

### What the 1830 Text Establishes (Alma 47:10-19)

1. **Identity**: Leader of Lamanite forces stationed on mount Antipas
2. **Temperament**: Described as cautious/fearful—refuses to come down when first summoned by Amalickiah
3. **Military status**: Commands a significant independent army
4. **Vulnerability**: Susceptible to ambitious co-commander Amalickiah's manipulations
5. **Betrayal mechanism**: Tricked into surrounding a group Amalickiah secretly favors
6. **Death**: Poisoned gradually ("by degrees") by Amalickiah's servant after serving his purpose
7. **Significance of death**: His death removes an obstacle to Amalickiah's rise to supreme power

### Narrative Functions Requiring Explanation
- Why is he portrayed as simultaneously strong (commands forces) and weak (easily manipulated)?
- Why the specific method: poison delivered gradually over time?
- Why the caution/wariness as a character trait?

## Narrative Archetype Analysis: Lehonti's Character Function

### The "Ambitious Lieutenant Betrayed" Archetype

Lehonti's story follows a classical motif: a military subordinate with legitimate command authority is manipulated by an ambitious rival, serves the rival's purposes, then is poisoned to prevent the subordinate from claiming equal or superior rank.

**Historical parallels to this archetype** (not necessarily named "Lehonti," but functionally similar):

#### 1. **Tanaghrisson (c. 1700-1754)** — Half-King of the Ohio Iroquois
- **Setting**: French & Indian War, Allegheny region
- **Role**: War chief commanding forces; initially cooperated with British/colonial forces
- **Betrayal mechanism**: Gradually sidelined by British commanders (especially Forbes in 1758)
- **Outcome**: Died under contested circumstances (poisoning rumored but unconfirmed in primary sources)
- **Similarity to Lehonti**: Cautious leader manipulated by colonial ambition; death preceded loss of authority
- **Dissimilarity**: Death less clearly attributable to deliberate poisoning; no clear analog to Amalickiah

#### 2. **The Catawba Confederation splits** (1750s-1760s)
- Multiple Catawba subchiefs (names like Ayres, Taylor, Hagler) were gradually isolated as British colonists played factions against each other
- **Similarity**: subordinate leaders outmaneuvered by rivals
- **Dissimilarity**: no specific "Lehonti" name; no clear poisoning narrative

#### 3. **Joseph Brant (Thayendanegea, 1743-1807)** — Mohawk Captain
- Notorious for orchestrating the removal of rivals who threatened his authority
- **Similarity**: ambitious military commander; disposed of competitors
- **Dissimilarity**: Brant was the poisoner/orchestrator, not the victim (opposite role from Lehonti)

#### 4. **Pontiac (1720-1769)**
- War chief of the Ottawa; coordinated an anti-colonial uprising
- Killed eventually by a rival's client (exact tribal identification disputed; possibly Peoria)
- **Similarity**: powerful war leader eliminated by smaller rivals
- **Dissimilarity**: killed in public setting, not by poison; the killing was interracial, not intra-nation

### Colonial Records: The "Poison Death in Captivity" Motif

**Finding**: Colonial military records do document instances of Native American leaders poisoned or suspected of poisoning in captivity/confinement, though the frequency is overstated in popular culture:

- **Opechancanough** (d. 1646, Powhatan Confederacy): shot in captivity, not poisoned
- **King Philip/Metacom** (d. 1676): killed in battle, not poisoned  
- **Cornstalk** (d. 1777, Shawnee): killed by American militia in captivity; not poisoned, killed by gunshot
- **Tecumseh** (d. 1813): killed in battle

**Actual documented poisoning cases** (minority of documented indigenous deaths):
- Various fur-trade context poisonings documented by Sleeper-Smith (*Indigenous Prosperity* in Great Lakes trade), but names and details are fragmentary
- No systematic roster of "leaders poisoned by political rivals" in accessible colonial sources

**Conclusion**: The poisoning-by-rival motif is not a dominant trope in colonial records, making it a less-likely indirect borrowing from a standard colonial-narrative source.

## Name Corruption / Variant Spellings in Colonial Records

### Could "Lehonti" Be a Colonist Misheard or Mistranscribed Name?

#### Known Examples of Colonial Name Corruption
- **Tecumseh**: transcribed as "Tecumtha," "Tecumtah," "Tomthly," "Big Knifer" (translations)
- **Corn Planter**: Ganus-yunteh → Colonel Cornplanter → "O'Bail"
- **Red Jacket**: Sagoyewatha → Red Jacket (literal translation)
- **Pontiac**: Obwandiyag → "Pontiac" (the French version; actual name lost to translation)

2. **Possible Lenape originals that might corrupt to "Lehonti"**

## Lahontan as a Potential Single Source for Both Liahona AND Lehonti

### The "One Source, Two Names" Hypothesis

Given that:
1. The original manuscript spelling of Lehonti is **"Lahonti"** (identical to Lahontan's spelling template)
2. Lahontan is already established as present in Jefferson's library and Library Company of Philadelphia
3. Both names share phonetic skeleton L-H-N-T (at least in original manuscript orthography)
4. No other historical figures named "Lehonti/Lahonti" found in colonial records

**Question**: Could Lahontan's text have influenced BOTH names through a single reading?

### Evaluation

**For**: 
- Parsimony: one source explains two names rather than requiring two separate derivations
- Orthographic consistency: both use the "Lahonti" / "Lahontan" proximity
- Availability: Lahontan confirmed in libraries accessible to early-republic milieu

**Against**:
- Functional mismatch: Lahontan's invented "Long River nations" don't include a character matching Lehonti's narrative role
- The compass/spirits passage (already identified as most-substantive Lahontan/Liahona parallel) doesn't obviously suggest a "betrayed military leader" character
- No narrative in Lahontan matches Lehonti's arc (cautious chief, manipulated into betrayal, poisoned)
- If Lahontan inspired both names, would we expect convergence in their narrative functions, not divergence?

**Interim assessment**: The "one source for two names" hypothesis is **suggestive but unresolved**. It requires either (1) identifying a Lahontan narrative element parallel to Lehonti's function that hasn't yet been recognized, or (2) demonstrating that small orthographic changes ("Lahonti" → "Lehonti") are within the pattern of transformation already established for "Lahontan" → "Liahona," making independent name-derivations from one source plausible.

## Alternative Origin Candidates

### If Not Lahontan, What Other Source Might Explain "Lehonti"?

#### 1. Biblical / Hebrew Etymology
- Hebrew *Levi* or *Levin* (the priestly tribe) — "Lev" means "heart"
- Could "Lehonti" be a transformation of "Levi" + suffix? (Levi + -honti?)
- **Problem**: No documented Hebrew word "-honti" or "-hanti"
- **Availability in 1820s**: Hebrew lexicons were accessible to educated readers
- **Functionality**: Levi as a tribal name doesn't match Lehonti's narrative role
- **Verdict**: Weak

#### 2. Greek/Latin Classical Names
- Latin *Leontes* (a figure in Greek mythology, the Spartan/Sicilian king from *Winter's Tale* fame—though Shakespeare's character, not ancient Greek)
- Phonetically: L-O-N-T-E-S → "Lehontes" → "Lehonti" (via suffix reduction)?
- **Availability**: Classical names were familiar in educated circles
- **Problem**: "Leontes" is a Shakespearean invention, not classical; no ancient Greek "Leontes" leader well-documented
- **Verdict**: Speculative but lower plausibility than Lahontan

#### 3. French Colonial Names
- French colonial documents often transcribed Native American names
- Could "Lehonti" be a French transcription of an Algonquian or Iroquoian name, then anglicized?
- **Example route**: French "Le-honti" or "Lehonty" → English "Lehonti"
- **Problem**: No French colonial source identifying "Lehonti" found yet
- **Verdict**: Possible but unverified

#### 4. Spalding's Own Invented Names
- Solomon Spalding's *Manuscript Story* included many invented character names
- Could Spalding have independently created "Lehonti" without a specific external source?
- **Supporting evidence**: Spalding's documented naming patterns (invented place names in *Manuscript Story*) show he created neologisms
- **Counter-evidence**: Other Book of Mormon character names show demonstrable external parallels (Alma → South American cultures, Moroni → coin/classical, Lehi → Arabic Hebrew, etc.)
- **Verdict**: Possible as an alternative to any specific source, but doesn't explain why—if randomly invented—the name happens to phonetically match Lahontan's spelling

## Documented Native American Figures Who Were Poisoned by Rivals

### Expanding Beyond Name-Match to Functional Parallels

If Lehonti's story follows the archetype of "cautious war leader poisoned by ambitious subordinate," are there documented historical examples?

#### Confirmed Poisonings

1. **Opechancanough's death (1646)** — Though killed by gunshot in captivity, not poison; orchestrated by English captors, not a rival from his own nation
2. **Pontiac's death (1769)** — Shot by a rival tribesman (identity unclear; various accounts blame Peoria or local foes), but in public setting, not gradual poisoning

#### Poisonings in Fur-Trade Context

Sleeper-Smith's research on Great Lakes fur-trade documents mentions instances where:
- Rival traders secretly poisoned competitors' Native American allies
- Poisoning by colonial rivals seeking to eliminate inconvenient indigenous leaders
- But these are fragmentary, lack named individuals, and differ from Lehonti's scenario (which is intra-nation, not a colonial/indigenous adversary dynamic)

#### No Clear Historical Analog

**Conclusion**: The specific trope of "indigenous war leader poisoned by his own ambitious subordinate" is **not a documented recurrent motif in accessible colonial military records**. If Lehonti's story is based on a historical model, it's either:
- From a source not widely circulated in colonial English records (French records? Spanish? Indigenous oral tradition?)
- From a fragmentary account not connected to a famous named figure
- An invented archetype drawn from Spalding's imagination or general literary knowledge

## Methodological Summary: What Would Constitute Evidence of a "Lehonti Origin Figure"?

For a claimed historical Lehonti prototype to be credible, we would need:

1. **Name match**: A documented Native American leader with a name phonetically similar to "Lehonti/Lahonti" in colonial records OR credible evidence of name corruption producing it
2. **Functional match**: A historical military leader whose story includes (a) cautious temperament, (b) command of significant forces, (c) betrayal by ambitious rival, (d) poisoning death
3. **Temporal match**: A figure active in a period plausibly within reach of early-1800s American cultural awareness (1700-1820s, allowing for 30-year cultural lag)
4. **Availability**: Documented evidence the figure was mentioned in texts circulating in Joseph Smith's formative environment (libraries, newspapers, schoolbooks, oral tradition)
5. **Functional specificity**: The parallel should be concrete and specific (not generic "military leader" tropes that apply to dozens of historical figures)

**Current status**: 
- **Name match**: Not found (zero documented NA leaders with "Lehonti/Lahonti" variant in accessible colonial records)
- **Functional match**: Weak (the "poisoned by ambitious rival" archetype is not a prominent documented trope, though individual instances exist)
- **Lahontan exception**: Lahontan himself is a real, documented figure; the orthographic match is strong; but his *narrative function* doesn't obviously parallel Lehonti's role

## EXECUTIVE FINDINGS

### CONVENTION PREVALENCE: **RARE**

Cardinal-direction naming (using compass directions as primary place-names) was **RARE** in 1700-1830 geographic literature when used as a standalone naming system. However, cardinal-directional LANGUAGE and geography organized around cardinal directions was COMMON in:
- Boundary descriptions ("ran from the sea east to the sea west")
- Geographic narrative ("to the north lay...")
- Speculative/unmapped region identification (the "Sea of the West")

**Nuance:** The convention was **COMMON as descriptive/organizational language** but **RARE as exclusive primary naming**.

---

### EARLIEST DOCUMENTED EXAMPLE

**Source:** Lahontan's *New Voyages to North America* (1703, English edition)  
**Usage:** Describes travel "northwestward" toward a salt lake, implying cardinal-direction geographic organization  
**Cartographic realization:** L'Isle's maps (1718+) incorporate the "Long River" account, creating the "Sea of the West" concept  
**Formal emergence:** 1718-1733 (L'Isle, Moll, Popple, Senex maps feature the concept explicitly)

---

### GEOGRAPHIC EXAMPLES FROM PERIOD MAPS

#### Example 1: "Sea of the West" (Lahontan-Derived, 1703-1780s)
- **Map sources:** Guillaume de l'Isle (1718, 1720), Hermann Moll (1720), Henry Popple (1733), John Senex (1720s-1750s)
- **How it appears:** Labeled as "Mer de l'Ouest" (French) or "Western Sea/Ocean" (English)
- **Naming pattern:** Feature identified by its cardinal direction relative to known geography
- **Prevalence:** Dominant on major maps 1720-1750; persists through 1780s

#### Example 2: Great Lakes Naming (Colonial Cartography, 1650-1750)
- **Features:** Lake Ontario, Erie, Huron, Michigan, Superior
- **Naming pattern:** Proper nouns (Indigenous-derived or explorer-named)
- **Cardinal directions used?** NO — lakes given distinct names, not "Lake North," "Lake East"
- **Implication:** When established names existed, cartographers preferred proper nouns

#### Example 3: Unmapped Interior Regions
- **How cartographers labeled unknown areas:** 
  - Blank space (no label)
  - "Unknown Regions" / "Lands Yet to Explore"
  - "Parts Unknown"
  - Question marks or empty cartouches
  - Sometimes: speculative direction-based description ("Supposed Lands to the Northwest")
- **Pattern:** Unmapped = sparse labeling; directional language only when position was the primary known fact

#### Example 4: Carver's Travels (1778)
- **Carver borrowed from Lahontan** but adapted naming conventions
- **His text:** Uses specific place-names and tribal designations (proper nouns) more prominently than directional naming
- **Editions:** 23+ circulations through 1830s, suggesting this naming style was preferred by publishers/readers

### TAHUGLAUK'S SALT LAKE (Lahontan, 1703)

**Primary source text (from *New Voyages*):**
- Lahontan describes finding a region to the northwest with a great salt lake associated with the "Tahuglauk" people
- The lake itself is not named "Sea of the North-West"
- Instead, it's identified by: (1) its tribal association (Tahuglauk), (2) its property (salt water), (3) its direction (northwest)
- **Naming verdict:** Lahontan used PROPER NOUN + DIRECTIONAL DESCRIPTOR, not pure cardinal-direction naming

### GREAT LAKES COLONIAL MAPPING

**Pattern established:** Colonial maps of the Great Lakes used:
- Indigenous/Algonquian names: Ontario, Erie, Huron, Michigan
- English descriptive names: Superior (for relative size/position)
- **NO cardinal-direction-only naming** for these features

**Implication:** The established geographic convention was proper-noun naming, even when features had directional associations

---

### CARVER'S GEOGRAPHIC NAMING (1778, 23+ Editions)

**From his *Travels through the Interior Parts of North America*:**
- Carver adopts specific place-names and tribal designations
- More consistent with proper-noun naming than pure directional naming
- The funeral-oration passage (borrowed from Lahontan) uses proper nouns and descriptive language
- **Verdict:** Carver reinforced proper-noun naming convention rather than promoting cardinal-direction naming

## BOOK OF MORMON GEOGRAPHIC NAMING ANALYSIS

### Text Examples (1830 Edition)
| Reference | Usage |
|-----------|-------|
| Helaman 3:8 | "sea south to the sea north, from the sea west to the sea east" |
| Helaman 3:8 | "ran from the sea east even to the sea west" |
| Alma various | Proper nouns used: Sidon (river), Rameumptom (region), Ripliancum (river) |

### The Unique Pattern

**Book of Mormon combines:**
1. **Named features** (Sidon, Rameumptom, Ripliancum) — using proper nouns
2. **Cardinal-direction boundaries** (sea east, sea west, sea north, sea south) — using directions only

**This hybrid system:**
- Uses four cardinal directions as **mutually exhaustive** geographic boundaries
- Uses four cardinal directions as **primary identifiers** (not secondary descriptors)
- Creates a complete geographic frame organized by compass points alone

### Comparative Rarity

**Comparison to period geography:**
- Great Lakes: Named with proper nouns (Ontario, Erie, Huron, Michigan, Superior)
- Speculative features: Often labeled by direction ("Sea of the West") but combined with other descriptors
- Travel narratives: Used directional language descriptively, not as exclusive naming system
- **Book of Mormon pattern:** Uses four cardinal directions as sole boundary identifiers — **NO EXACT PARALLEL FOUND**

## ASSESSED UNIQUENESS VERDICT

### Book of Mormon's "Four Seas" Usage

**Against "standard for era":**
- The pattern of using four cardinal directions simultaneously as exhaustive geographic boundaries was NOT standard
- Standard convention was: proper nouns (Great Lakes) or property-based names (Salt Lake, White River)
- When directions were used, they were typically descriptive (e.g., "western territories") not naming

**Against "unusual":**
- The pattern is somewhat unusual but not entirely without precedent
- The "Sea of the West" concept demonstrates that directional geographic organization existed
- Geographic narratives commonly used directional language

**Against "unique":**
- No exact parallel to using four cardinal seas simultaneously as the ONLY boundary identifiers has been found
- However, research is incomplete — sources not yet fully examined

### VERDICT: **UNUSUAL** to **UNIQUE**

The Book of Mormon's specific use of four cardinal direction-based geographic boundaries is:

**Most likely: UNUSUAL**
- It represents a deliberate, systematic geographic organizing principle
- It differs from the more common proper-noun and property-based naming conventions
- It resembles the "Sea of the West" cartographic convention (directional geographic organization)
- But no exact match in contemporaneous literature has been found

**Possibly: UNIQUE**  
- If systematic searches of 1700-1830 geographic literature reveal no other texts using this exact four-directional system
- The current research gap (especially regarding Lahontan's own text, Morse's textbook, and periodical literature) means uniqueness cannot be definitively established yet

## FINAL ASSESSMENT

### Convention Prevalence
**RARE** — when used as primary naming system; **COMMON** when used as descriptive/organizational language

### Earliest Documented Example  
**1703** — Lahontan's *New Voyages* (describing travel directions toward salt lake)  
**1718** — L'Isle's cartographic realization (explicit "Sea of the West" concept)

### Examples
1. **Sea of the West** (Lahontan-derived, L'Isle/Moll/Popple/Senex maps, 1718-1750s)
2. **Travel narrative directions** (Lahontan, Carver, and other explorers describing territory by compass directions)
3. **Geographic boundary descriptions** (periodical and geographic literature using "from east to west, north to south")

### Book of Mormon Usage Uniqueness
**UNUSUAL to UNIQUE**

The simultaneous use of four cardinal direction-names ("sea north," "sea south," "sea east," "sea west") as exhaustive geographic boundaries appears:
- **Not standard for the era** (proper nouns dominated)
- **Unusual as a naming system** (directional naming existed but was less common as primary identifier)
- **Possibly unique** (no exact parallel yet found, but research incomplete)

**Conclusion:** The convention was rare enough and specific enough to suggest either:
1. Influence from a source emphasizing directional geographic organization (Lahontan/"Sea of the West" maps/descriptions)
2. Or independent invention, which would itself be unusual for the era

## METHODOLOGICAL NOTES

This research demonstrates that the "four seas" naming convention in the Book of Mormon:
- Is **not a generic or universal geographic convention** of the era
- **Resembles specific 18th-century cartographic concepts** (especially the "Sea of the West" hypothesis)
- **Requires specific explanation** — either from a documented source (Lahontan, cartographic maps, geographic textbooks) or as independent/revelatory origin

The burden of explanation falls on whichever theory is proposed. A generic "it was just how people named geography then" is insufficient — the evidence shows it was not.
